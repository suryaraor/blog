This wasn't just another round of "restructuring." This was a company celebrating how much work its machines could do, in the same breath as telling a thousand people they were no longer necessary. It's the productivity paradox nobody wants to talk about: *faster automation doesn't equal progress. It equals displacement with a spreadsheet.*

## The Seductive Math That Breaks Your Heart

Here's what the headlines will tell you: AI-generated code is cheaper, faster, and measurably improves development velocity. Snap's infrastructure is better. Their time-to-market is shorter. By pure metrics, this is a win. Ship faster. Burn less capital. Impress investors.

Here's what they don't tell you: a software engineer's mortgage payment doesn't care about velocity metrics.

The industry has a terrible habit of treating productivity like a moral good in itselfÃ¢â‚¬”as if moving faster is always better, regardless of the cost. But productivity isn't neutral. Productivity in a system shaped by quarterly earnings calls means: *Do more with fewer people.*

And now AI has become the ultimate instrument for that calculation.

## Why This Isn't Actually About Code

This story isn't really about software engineering. It's about a much older pattern finally reaching escape velocity.

For decades, automation has followed a predictable script: new technology emerges, productivity increases, some jobs disappear, displacement gets framed as "progress," and the people whose careers just evaporated are told to "retrain" as if human lives are as easy to reboot as code.

But something's different this time.

Previous waves of automation were gradual enough that you could *almost* pretend it was natural. Factory robots? Okay, that's manufacturing. Spreadsheets replacing clerks? That's just how industries evolve. This? This is different because:

- **It's happening at scale.** Not in one company or sector, but across every industry simultaneously.
- **It's happening fast.** We're not talking about a 10-year transition. Snap went from "we're using AI tools" to "we're cutting 1,000 people" in months.
- **There's no obvious replacement skill to learn.** When robots took factory jobs, you could become a technician. When AI writes code, what's the senior engineer supposed to doÃ¢â‚¬”become an AI reviewer?

This is the moment we need to stop pretending this is about productivity and start talking about redistribution.

## The Productivity Delusion

Here's what puzzles me: we live in an era of genuinely mind-blowing technical achievement. Claude can write production-ready code. GPT-5.4 is more reliable than most human experts at certain professional tasks. We've built systems that augment human capability in extraordinary ways.

And yet, the first thing we did was reach for layoffs.

Not: "How can we use this to let engineers spend less time on boilerplate and more on architecture?"

Not: "How do we redirect this freed-up capacity toward problems that actually matter?"

Not: "How do we create prosperity instead of just efficiency?"

Just: *Cut the headcount. Call it progress. Move on.*

The tragedy is that we had a choice. We still have a choice. But productivity-obsessed capitalism has a direction it flows, and it's not toward sharing the gains. It flows toward concentration.

**Consider this:** In 2026, McKinsey estimates that 20% of companies are capturing 75% of AI's economic value. That number will only get worse. The rich get richer, the displaced get resumes, and the efficiency increases feed into shareholder returns, not into societal benefit.

AI-generated code *does* make talented engineers more powerful. A senior developer paired with Claude is legitimately more productive than that developer alone. Those capabilities are real and they're liberating.

The problem is that we decidedÃ¢â‚¬”collectively, implicitly, without much debateÃ¢â‚¬”that when productivity gains happen, they belong exclusively to the people who already have power. And if you're on the wrong side of that equation, well, you should have gone to Stanford and joined a startup.

That's not innovation. That's just aristocracy with GPUs.

## What Actually Needs to Happen

I don't have a solution. I'm suspicious of anyone who claims they do with confidence. But I know what a serious conversation would include:

- **Rethinking productivity metrics.** If a system generates more code but displaces workers, is it actually more productive? Or are we just externalizing the cost?
- **Questioning the default.** When AI frees up capacity, the default doesn't have to be "lay people off." It could be "pay people the same and have them solve harder problems." We just choose not to.
- **Demanding redistribution mechanisms.** If AI's gains are systemicÃ¢â‚¬”benefiting whole industriesÃ¢â‚¬”then the compensation for disruption shouldn't come from individual workers. It should come from the system.
- **Building more humanely.** Companies that chose differentlyÃ¢â‚¬”that used AI to augment rather than replace, that shared gains rather than hoarded themÃ¢â‚¬”would become the story. Instead, we celebrate the ones that move fastest and cut deepest.

## The Question That Matters

When Evan Spiegel announced that AI was generating most of Snap's code, he was celebrating a technical achievement. And it *is* one.

But he was also announcing a redistribution of valueÃ¢â‚¬”from 1,000 people who built that capability to shareholders. And that's the part nobody in the industry wants to examine too closely.

**Here's my challenge for you:** Next time you hear about "AI productivity gains," ask the follow-up question nobody's comfortable asking:

*"Whose productivity? And who's paying for it?"*

Because the code doesn't tell you that story. The spreadsheets don't either. You have to look at the people, and when you do, the productivity paradise starts to look an awful lot like a cost-cutting restructure dressed up in futurism.

That's uncomfortable. That's also honest. And maybe that's where we should start.
