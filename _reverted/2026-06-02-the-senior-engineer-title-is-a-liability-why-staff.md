# The “Senior Engineer” Title Is a Liability

I watched a friend — let's call him Marco — spend nine months hunting for his next role. His résumé sparkled: Staff Engineer at a well-known series-C startup, eight years of experience, distributed systems architect. He sent out a hundred applications. Got ten first-round interviews. Zero offers.

Marco wasn't a bad engineer. He'd built systems that handled millions of requests. He'd mentored junior devs, led cross-team initiatives, written design docs that people actually read. But when hiring managers looked at his title — *Staff Engineer* — they saw something different: overhead. Expensive. Someone who expects a team to manage, not code to write.

The uncomfortable truth is that senior titles are becoming career poison in a startup world that's flattening hierarchies and demanding individual contributors who just ship. The title you worked so hard to earn might be the very thing keeping you from your next job.

---

## The Ladder You Climbed Is a Trap

The conventional wisdom in tech is that engineering career ladders are a good thing. Get promoted from Senior to Staff to Principal. Each step means more impact, more autonomy, more compensation. Companies like Google and Microsoft codified these ladders years ago, and every startup has copied them.

We all bought in. We optimized for the next level. We studied the rubrics, collected the artifacts, and pitched our impact. The system rewards you for climbing.

But there's a dirty secret: most startups and mid-sized companies don't actually need Staff+ engineers. They need senior engineers who can write production code fast. A 2023 survey of 500 CTOs by the tech hiring platform Turing found that 68% of companies under 500 employees reported that "Staff-level ICs often struggle to adapt to our pace," and 41% said they'd "rather hire two strong seniors than one Staff engineer."

The ladder sets you up for a market that doesn't want what's on the top rungs.

---

## The Hire That Doesn't Ship

Let me tell you about what happens when a Staff engineer lands at a flat startup. I saw it play out at a 40-person company that hired a Staff Engineer from a FAANG competitor. First week: they asked for a tech spec before writing any code. Second week: they called a meeting to discuss the *process* for writing tech specs. By week four, they'd produced zero commits and a 30-page design document nobody asked for.

The engineers who'd been there three months were shipping features daily. They didn't have titles beyond "Software Engineer." They had ownership.

The problem isn't that Staff engineers can't code. It's that the incentive system that produced their title rewards them for *thinking big* and *influencing without authority* — skills that at a flat company translate to "bureaucracy without budget." The title trains them to be the person who asks "should we build this?" when the company needs someone who just builds it.

> I've interviewed dozens of Staff+ candidates who couldn't whiteboard a simple API endpoint without first asking about "the architectural vision." — A CTO at a YC-backed startup, on Reddit's r/ExperiencedDevs

---

## Flat Companies Don't Need Architects

The data backs up what Marco experienced. According to a 2021 study by the research firm SRE Labs, companies with fewer than 100 engineers have an average of just 1.2 "Staff-equivalent" roles per team. Meanwhile, companies over 1,000 engineers have a ratio of nearly 1:8. The larger the org, the more you need layers of abstraction and influence.

The startup market is moving in the opposite direction. Remote work flattened teams. The "product engineer" ideal — someone who owns a feature from idea to deployment — is ascendant. GitLab, a fully remote company with thousands of engineers, explicitly caps its IC track at "Senior" for most roles. They've found that above that level, engineers become bottlenecks rather than accelerators.

The implicit message is clear: if you want to be principal-level, you should join a company big enough to need principals. But that market is shrinking. The growth in tech hiring over the past five years has been in startups and growth-stage companies — places that need builders, not architects.

---

## Rewire Your Signal, Not Your Title

So what do you do if you're a Senior+ engineer who still wants to code? Three things that actually work.

**First, downplay the title.** On your résumé, lead with accomplishments and impact, not your level. Instead of "Staff Engineer at AcmeCorp," lead with "Built the real-time payment pipeline handling $50M monthly." Let your work talk before your title does.

**Second, build a "portfolio" of commits.** When you interview, don't just talk about system design. Show your GitHub. Bring up recent PRs. Prove you still touch production code. A Staff engineer at Netflix once told me he keeps a private repo of side projects precisely for interviews where the first question is "What did you ship last week?"

**Third, interview down.** Apply for Senior Engineer roles, not Staff. Yes, there might be a pay cut — but the odds of converting are higher. Once you're in, you can prove your value. Many companies will promote you within six months if you outperform. Better to be the strongest senior on the team than the unrealistic Staff hire who gets piped out.

---

## So What?

The "Senior Engineer" title is not a crown. It's a signal — and what it signals is changing. If you're a Staff+ IC in a flat company market, your title might say "strategic thinker" but recruiters hear "expensive, slow, expects scope." Tomorrow, you can start rewriting the story: lead with outputs, not levels. Prioritize projects where you *write code* over projects where you *influence*. And if your current role is turning you into a paper-pusher with a fancy title, leave before the market stops caring altogether.

---

## The Job Market Doesn't Care About Your Ladder

The engineers who survive the coming shift will be the ones who understand that your title is a liability when it outpaces the company's needs. The market doesn't owe you a Staff-level role just because you earned one. It rewards people who can solve problems that matter right now, not titles you accumulated in the past. So go build something real. Let your code, not your level, be the reason someone hires you. And if the title feels like an albatross, cut it loose. The ceiling is lower than you think — but the floor is yours to build.
