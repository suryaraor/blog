# We're Solving AI's Efficiency Problem. Then Ignoring It.

Last week, Google researchers published a paper describing an algorithm that sounds like science fiction: TurboQuant can compress an AI model's memory requirements by six times without losing a single percentage point of accuracy. Think about that for a second. Not five times. Not four. Six times. The Key-Value cache-the computational bottleneck that's been choking AI performance for years-reduced to just 3 bits of precision. Meanwhile, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite is hitting the market at a quarter the price of its predecessors, 2.5 times faster, with 45% quicker outputs.

We just solved efficiency.

Then Meta announced they're spending $115 to $135 billion on compute infrastructure this year. Double what they spent last year.

If you're not feeling the cognitive dissonance yet, sit with it for a moment. We've discovered ways to make AI dramatically leaner, faster, and cheaper. The engineering is elegant. The results are real. And the industry's response is basically: *Great. Now let's build bigger.*

This isn't incompetence. It's something more interesting-and more unsettling.

## The Efficiency Breakthrough Everyone's Quietly Celebrating

Let's talk about what actually happened, because the victories here are genuine. When TurboQuant came out of Google's research division, it wasn't incremental progress. Reducing memory consumption by 6x without accuracy loss is the kind of thing that should fundamentally change how we build these systems.

> Quantization-throwing away precision-used to mean throwing away performance. Not anymore.

Before this, we believed there was a hard trade-off. You wanted speed? You had to lose accuracy. You wanted memory efficiency? The model got dumber. These weren't bugs; they seemed like physics. But the latest generation of research is dismantling that assumption entirely.

Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite embodies this shift. It's not a crippled version of the flagship model. It's a rethinking of the entire architecture. Smaller inference costs. Faster responses. The kind of efficiency that should make accessibility advocates cheer. If you can run state-of-the-art AI on cheaper infrastructure, you democratize it. Right? Theoretically, yes. In practice, we'll see.

## The Spending Spree That Contradicts Everything

Here's where the story gets weird.

While researchers are publishing efficiency breakthroughs, OpenAI is crossing $25 billion in annualized revenue and taking steps toward a public listing. Anthropic is at $19 billion. Google's already a public company burning cash on AI like it's going out of style. And then there's Meta, which just announced they're doubling their AI capex.

$115 to $135 billion. For infrastructure. For compute. For the exact thing we just learned how to use less of.

The explanation you'll hear is that scale matters more than efficiency. And it's not wrong. A 6x efficiency gain is incredible until you want a system that's 100x more capable. Then you're right back in the same boat, but with a bigger boat. OpenAI's GPT-5.4 with "Thinking" variants and test-time compute requires more not less infrastructure. You can't think your way out of that.

But here's what bothers me: We're not just building bigger for capabilities we need. We're building bigger because that's what we know how to do. Building efficiently is harder. Building bigger is easier. Spend more, scale faster, win the race. Efficiency is a research achievement. Scaling is a business strategy.

## The Problem With Ignoring Your Own Solutions

Let me frame this another way. Imagine you're in a burning building. Engineers create an innovative new exit route that reduces evacuation time from one hour to ten minutes. Everyone celebrates. Then, instead of using it, the facility managers decide to... expand the building. Sure, there's technically more exit capacity now, but it's the same congestion, just with a bigger footprint.

That's not a perfect analogy, but it captures something real about the industry's mixed signals.

The inefficiency we just solved wasn't a technical problem. It was a choice. A systems problem. And we're solving it at the research level while doubling down on the behavior that created it in the first place.

**Three uncomfortable observations:**

- Efficiency breakthroughs don't reduce capex if you're trying to maximize performance at any cost
- Smaller models are more accessible, but only if companies have incentive to commercialize them (spoiler: margins are smaller)
- The sustainability narrative in AI keeps bumping into the profitability narrative, and profitability wins every single time

## So What? Why You Should Actually Care

You might be thinking: "These are trillion-dollar companies' problems, not mine."

Wrong.

If the industry decides efficiency is solved and therefore doesn't matter, the cost curve doesn't flatten-it reflects the spending curve. Anthropic approaching $19 billion in revenue. Meta spending $115 billion on infrastructure. These aren't abstract numbers. They're decisions about what kind of AI gets built, who gets to build it, and what kind of economic moat gets established around it.

When efficiency improvements don't translate to lower costs for end users and smaller organizations, you get consolidation. You get dominance by whoever can afford to spend the most. You get a world where building AI is only viable if you're already absurdly profitable.

This is how you go from "AI will democratize everything" to "four companies control the future."

The real win wouldn't be TurboQuant in a research paper. It would be TurboQuant in every open-source model, every startup's infrastructure, every research lab's pipeline. It would be efficiency gains that actually change the cost structure. Instead, we're watching efficiency breakthroughs get absorbed into a bigger, more expensive infrastructure race.

## The Call to Adventure

Here's what I'm wondering: If you work in AI-whether you're a researcher, an engineer, a decision-maker, or someone building products on top of these systems-what would it actually take for efficiency to matter more than scale?

Not in research papers. In real investment decisions. In resource allocation. In the systems you're building right now.

Some of you will answer: "It already does." And if your company is optimizing for efficiency, I genuinely want to hear about it. Because the mainstream narrative suggests otherwise.

The uncomfortable truth is that we've solved the technical problem of efficiency. The remaining problem is entirely a social one. It's about incentives, competition, and what we collectively decide is worth building toward.

We cracked the code. Now we need to decide if we actually want to use it.
